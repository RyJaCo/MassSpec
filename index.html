Why equimolar peptides are not seen equally

Every peptide cut from one protein molecule is present in the same molar amount. The signal an instrument records for each one still differs by orders of magnitude, because the measured intensity depends on the peptide as well as on how much of it there is:

  • Ionisation efficiency. Basic, hydrophobic peptides pick up protons and reach the gas phase more easily in electrospray than acidic, hydrophilic ones.
  • Chromatography. Very hydrophilic peptides wash through the trap column; very hydrophobic ones elute late, broadly, or not at all. Co-eluting peptides compete for charge (ion suppression).
  • Charge state and modifications. Signal is split across 2+, 3+ and modified forms (Met oxidation, deamidation), so each precursor carries only part of the total.
  • Incomplete digestion. Missed cleavages divert material into longer peptides.
  • Fragmentation and identification. A peptide only counts as detected if its fragments score well enough to pass the FDR threshold, and very short peptides are often not unique to one protein.

This is why label-free protein quantification compares the same peptide across samples, and never compares different peptides with each other as if they were equimolar.
